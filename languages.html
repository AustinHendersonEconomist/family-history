Two branches of one family · from the 1600s to the 1800s

The family's mother tongues

Before everyone ended up speaking American English, the family’s ancestors arrived speaking at least a dozen languages and dialects: Mohawk, Dutch, Norwegian, Danish, Scots, Gaelic, several kinds of English, and half a dozen distinct German dialects. This page groups them by language and says who spoke what, and where they came from.

Iroquoian

Mohawk

Kanien'kéha, “the language of the people of the flint”

Mohawk is a Northern Iroquoian language, related to Oneida, Onondaga, Cayuga and Seneca. In the 1600s the Mohawk lived in palisaded towns along the Mohawk River west of present-day Schenectady, and traded furs with the Dutch at Fort Orange (Albany). Mohawk is still spoken today and is being actively revived.

West Germanic

Dutch, and New York “Low Dutch”

Nederlands; in colonial New York, simply “Duits” or Low Dutch

The first settlers of Albany and Schenectady spoke the Dutch of Holland. Their American-born grandchildren kept a local dialect, New York Dutch, alive for over a century after the English took over in 1664. The Schoharie church still wrote baptisms in Dutch in the 1790s. Susanna Becker was christened in 1794 as “Susanna Bekker”, daughter of “Ludewig”.

New York Dutch left American English words like cookie (koekje), boss (baas), stoop (stoep) and Santa Claus (Sinterklaas).

North Germanic

Norwegian and Danish

Norwegian

In the 1600s Norway was ruled from Copenhagen, and its written language was Danish. People spoke Norwegian dialects, though. Fredrikstad, at the mouth of the Glomma River in Østfold, spoke an East Norwegian dialect. A surprising number of New Netherland's settlers were Norwegian sailors and timbermen.

  • SNO
    Arent Andriessen Bradt tree tree
    born about 1615 · Fredrikstad, Østfold, Norway
    9th-great-grandfather
    Norwegian (Østfold dialect), and he would have learned Dutch at Rensselaerswyck. Norwegians in the colony were often nicknamed “de Noorman”.

Danish

Danish dialects differ sharply between Jutland and the islands, and Fred's home region isn't known yet. In the 1880s, emigrants left both for American cities and for the farm country of the upper Midwest.

Danish is known for its stød, a catch in the voice that can be the only difference between two words.

West Germanic

German, in six dialects

“German” in the 1700s and 1800s wasn't one spoken language. A Swabian and a Saxon could struggle to understand each other's speech, and Standard German was mostly for writing and church. The German-speaking ancestors are all on the Snouse and Ashmead side, and they came from at least six distinct dialect areas.

Swabian (Schwäbisch)

An Alemannic dialect of Württemberg. Emmingen sits above the Nagold valley at the edge of the northern Black Forest, in the heart of Swabian country.

Swabian is famous for its diminutive -le: Häusle for “little house”, and Spätzle, the noodles.

Upper Saxon (Obersächsisch)

The speech of the Kingdom of Saxony (Dresden, Leipzig, Meissen). Written Standard German grew largely out of the Meissen chancery language that Luther used, but the spoken dialect is quite different and is often teased elsewhere in Germany. If the Snouses came from the Prussian Province of Saxony rather than the Kingdom, their speech could have been Thuringian or East Low German instead. The census says only “Saxony”.

Alsatian (Elsässisch), and its northern cousin

Alsace was ruled by France from the 1600s, but villagers there spoke German dialects, with French only among officials and the elite. Most of Alsace speaks Low Alemannic, close to the speech of Baden across the Rhine. The far north, beyond the Haguenau forest around Wissembourg, speaks a Franconian dialect much closer to the Palatine German next door.

Palatine German (Pfälzisch) and its neighbours

The Rhine Franconian dialects of the Palatinate and southern Hesse. The 1709–10 refugees to London and New York were called “Palatines” whatever their exact origin. In America, the Palatine dialect is the main ancestor of Pennsylvania Dutch.

Swiss German: Bernese Oberland, and Baden's Alemannic

The high Alemannic dialects of Switzerland and the far south-west of Germany. The Kandertal is an Alpine valley of the Bernese Oberland. Its speech is among the most conservative German dialects, and hard for other Germans to follow.

Pennsylvania Dutch (Deitsch)

The German that formed in colonial Pennsylvania and Maryland from Palatine, Alsatian and Swiss speech. Here “Dutch” comes from Deitsch, meaning German, not from Holland. The American-born Slifers, Browns, Millers and Biglers of York County, Pennsylvania, and Frederick County, Maryland, probably spoke it into the early 1800s, before the move to Ohio. Some families also came from Germantown, settled in 1683 by German and Dutch Mennonites, including the papermaking Rittenhouse family on the Smith side.

West Germanic

Scots

Lowland Scots: the language of Robert Burns

Scots is a sister language of English, growing from the same Old English roots. In the 1700s and 1800s it was the everyday speech of the Scottish Lowlands, while English was for church and writing. The Scots ancestors came from at least four of its dialect regions.

Everyday Scots words: bairn (child), ken (know), kirk (church), dreich (dreary weather).

Celtic

Scottish Gaelic and Irish

Scottish Gaelic (Gàidhlig)

The Highland language. Kintyre was a frontier where Gaelic met the Lowland Scots planted around Campbeltown in the 1600s. As late as 1794 about two-thirds of Campbeltown's people still spoke Gaelic.

Irish (Gaeilge), and the English of Ireland and Ulster

In the 1700s and early 1800s Irish was still the language of most of rural Ireland, especially the west and north-west. Protestant settlers, Quakers and town families mostly spoke English, or in Ulster, Ulster Scots. For each person below, their likely speech depends as much on community and religion as on place.

Many Irish surnames are translations: Ward is Mac an Bhaird, “son of the bard”, a famous family of hereditary poets in Donegal.

West Germanic

English, from many counties

The English immigrants on both sides came from very different dialect regions. A Lancashire weaver, a Wiltshire farmer and a London shipwright of 1680 would each have sounded distinct. The Quakers among them also used “plain speech”: thee and thou to everyone, and numbered months instead of names like January.

When did each family switch to English?

  • The Henderson side was English-speaking almost throughout. The exceptions are Fred Peterson (Danish, came in 1883), the 1700s Scots and Irish settlers, and possibly John Manack.
  • New York Dutch (Vrooman, Bradt, Van Slyck, Hagedoorn) lasted about 150 years in America, fading in Schoharie around 1800. Lorinda Multer (born 1838) grew up speaking English.
  • Pennsylvania Dutch (Slifer, Brown, Miller, Bigler) faded once the families moved to Ohio in the early 1800s.
  • Swabian and Saxon German (Huber, Snouse) were spoken in Greenville, Ohio, from the 1840s. Harry Snouse, born 1874, was probably the first in his line to speak only English.
  • Ayrshire Scots came over with the McMasters in 1852. Their Illinois-born children, including Mary E. McMaster (born 1877), spoke American English, perhaps with a few Scots words at home.
